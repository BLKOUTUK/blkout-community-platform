A note from Rob
Members will drive this organisation. Our job is to meet you where you are.

BLKOUT began in the summer of 2016 with a Facebook post and a hunch: that Black gay, bi and trans men in this country needed somewhere to find each other that we owned ourselves. Ten years on, the hunch has held up better than some of the structures we built to test it. The first company was wound up in July 2024. On 24 November 2025 we registered again — this time as a Community Benefit Society, owned by its members, with an asset lock that means what we build stays with the community it was built for.

Why start again as a co-operative? Because a community platform that answers to a founder, or to a funder, is only ever borrowing its community's trust. Ownership changes the question from "what will they do for us?" to "what will we do together?" That is the organisation we are building, and this is the report of its first year.

It has been a year of foundations, and deliberately so. Getting the governance right. Putting the digital framework in place. They are what allow us to respond to members rather than guess at them, and what will let every pound we raise go into our capacity to hear your concerns and act on your needs. We have moved, as adrienne maree brown puts it, at the speed of trust — because trust is the one thing a community organisation can neither rush nor do without. Members will drive this organisation. Our job is to meet you where you are.

What follows sets out what we built this year, and what we are building next. We always knew this would be a marathon rather than a sprint.

Rob Berkeley, one of BLKOUT's founders

Who we are

BLKOUT is the Black Queer Men's Liberation Collective. We host gatherings, build space for dialogue, and create community-owned technology for the health, wealth and happiness of Black gay, bi and trans men across the UK. Through local chapters, cooperative membership, creative events, shared learning, and digital media, we turn connection into individual well-being and collective power — owned by and accountable to the people we serve.

A Community Benefit Society registered with the FCA (RS009639), with an asset lock.

This year · 1 of 3

An intervention that works

The first test of any community organisation is simple. Does it do something people want, and does it change anything for them?

Ivor's Compass · panel art

Ivor's Compass

In April we opened Ivor's Compass at Stanley Arts in South Norwood — a heritage project about Ivor Cummings, the Black queer civil servant who welcomed the Windrush in 1948, funded by Croydon's Samuel Coleridge-Taylor 150 programme. Seven interview tables, where people sat with each other's stories. Three commissioned writers. Seven illustrated panels. The companion journal is still open at compass.blkoutuk.com, and still being written in.

After the picnic · the Zodiac · 16 August

Gathering

In August we took over a corner of Regent's Park for the summer picnic, then carried on into karaoke at the Zodiac. In February, for LGBT History Month, we were on a panel at a University of the Arts London event organised by Jason Forrest, alongside Lady Phyll Opoku-Gyimah of UK Black Pride. We spoke with Black Thrive (April), ran a workshop on Critical Frequency for Action for Race Equality (June), and joined LGBTQ+ Global Majority Week (August).

BLKOUT Local · Brent · Wembley

Local everywhere

By the end of September BLKOUT Local had twelve local doors open across London — one page per borough, gathering what is happening for Black queer men nearby, checked with people who live there. A practical answer to a big question: where are we, and how do we find each other?

Media

The newsletter goes to 1,449 people. Every week AIvor, our AI presenter, brings the community its news digest on YouTube and Instagram — and what people ask AIvor on our sites tells us what they need. OOMF lets anyone put themselves in the story as a comic-book hero. And our events calendar, checked by hand, keeps a running record of what's on, what's open and where we are.

This year · 2 of 3

Sustainable and secure

A community that is going to trust us with its stories, its data and eventually its subscriptions deserves an organisation that has done its homework. This is the work I am proudest of.

Governance

We adopted our Rules and appointed a board of six directors. Over the year the board met with papers circulated in advance, and adopted a Safeguarding Policy covering adults, children and young people (June); an AI policy (17 June), published in full; Financial Regulations (26 August); and a register of interests — then carried out its first self-appraisal (September).

Money

Our first accounts run from registration on 24 November 2025 to 30 September 2026. Income was £2,500.00 and spending £2,421.12, leaving net assets of £78.88. Every penny is accounted for: reconciled to the bank statement, with the books kept under version control so every change can be traced. We are insured (Zurich, from August) and registered with HMRC.

Funding

We applied to funders throughout the year, and before it closed we won our first grant as a co-operative: £1,500 from Croydon Loves You for Making Ourselves From Scratch. We also wrote to two national funders to make a point that affects us directly. Funds that only support organisations based in a particular place shut out communities organised by identity rather than postcode — communities like ours, spread across a city and a country.

Automation and AI — deliberate first steps

BLKOUT is run day to day by one person. Automation is how that is possible at all: scheduled routines that publish, collect and remind; one register for everything we post; a newsletter that builds itself from the month's work; our figures gathered automatically each month; replies to people who get in touch.

We use AI openly, and we wrote down how before we went further. The AI policy is published in full at commons.blkoutuk.com/tech, with a box for your response. Its principles include "a human answers for everything", "we say what's synthetic" and "consent is the line between care and surveillance". Judgement and care stay with people.

A digital commons

If we are building community-owned technology, it should not stop at our own door. In May we opened commons.blkoutuk.com, with an invitation to peer organisations and a short film about what we might share. The conversations it started — with DiaspoRainbow, at a peer gathering in late May, with Black Thrive — are under way, and the offer stands. Different, not worse.

Keeping your data safe

We are not operating in a static space. In August we found a potential threat — an export of BLKOUTHUB members' details that people who should not have seen it could have reached. We assessed it, closed it, recorded it and reported it to the board. It was a sharp reminder of how much our security matters, and we spent the rest of the summer hardening the platform.

That matters more now than it did a year ago. Internationally there are concerted attempts to roll back our progress — to deny our existence, and to deny our humanity. A community facing that has to be able to trust whoever holds its information. We intend to deserve that trust.

This year · 3 of 3

Advancing liberation

Liberation is the long game. We are building for it deliberately, at the speed that trust allows: some of it is already built, and the rest is designed and in order.

What is built

The democratic machinery is real: members' proposals and votes are built into the platform, with access rules enforced. Membership itself opens on 28 December — Joseph Beam Day — a date the board chose for its meaning. And our magazine has a name: BLKOUT Review.

What we build next

We have designed the pieces that give our community real control over its own work and data, and they come next: creator sovereignty, so contributors control how their work is used; a review process for cultural authenticity; care for people's wellbeing built into how we collect data; and AIvor's commitments made enforceable rather than written down. We name them here so you can hold us to them.

Remaking connections

This year we went back out to the people and organisations we work alongside. Two of us joined the DiaspoRainbow retreat at Bidston in May — twelve people, twenty-four recorded reflections, held in an app we built for the weekend. In July we answered DiaspoRainbow's partner consultation and offered the QTIPOC CookOut.

Critical Frequency

Critical Frequency began as a question about mental health and media. In March it sharpened into something more political: the problem is not our methods but our invisibility in the data systems that decide where help goes. We took that argument to potential partners in May, to Action for Race Equality in June and to Professor Kevin Fenton in August. Our letter to funders about place-based eligibility was our first advocacy in this space in our own name. It will not be the last.

Voices

BLKOUT Voices is where we think out loud again. "Ten reasons Instagram ain't the one", an essay and film published in August, made the case for owning our own channels — a case the board acted on when it decided to leave X.

Making sense of the archive

Ten years of BLKOUT's own record are now searchable and usable. We have designed THE ACCOUNT, the society's record of its own acts, so that what we do is kept and can be checked. Everyone who has written for us since 2016 has kept their copyright; that will not change.

Digital sovereignty

We reviewed who we know and how we hold it: 204 UK Black queer organisations mapped, 37 Black queer creators identified, our contact records cleaned up. A community that holds its own map of itself — rather than renting it from a platform that can change the rules overnight — has a kind of power it did not have before.

A board growing into its role

The board's first year has been about governing a co-operative that is still building itself: reading papers before meeting, adopting policy with care, declaring interests, and assessing its own effectiveness.

What we learned

Never a failure, always a lesson

The right tools carry a long way

Automation took one operator further than anyone would expect of an organisation our size. The next stretch comes from people and money.

Where funders draw lines matters

Place-based funding rules exclude communities like ours by design rather than intent. Saying so out loud is part of our work.

Trust is built at its own speed

We put governance and security before growth, deliberately, so that the organisation is ready for members when they arrive.

Next year · what happens next

Building on year one

The foundations are laid. The next phase turns them towards you. The money we raise goes into our capacity to listen and respond; your concerns and needs set the direction; and we come to where you are rather than asking you to come to us. The same three threads, because this is one build.
